As Lead Technical Artist, my work on Kong: Skull Island spanned two sides: planning the technical art for the entire title, and engineering the graphics behind the character. The title was a Unity-to-Godot port, so much of it meant faithfully recreating the original's look in a new engine — but day to day, the work was the shaders, the fur, and the pipeline that held it all together.

## The technical-art blueprint

As Lead Technical Artist, I was responsible for the title's technical-art blueprint. In Jira I scoped, planned, and mapped out the full framework — 60+ custom shaders, material systems, 3D models, textures, VFX pipelines, and the core engine dependencies that tied them together. The plan established a modular, theme-swapping pipeline so the game's visual theme could shift dynamically during play — art assets, visual effects, and lighting all swapping as the theme changed between bonuses, each with its own look. A lot of the work was bridging creative direction with rigid technical constraints: setting clear asset specifications, organizing cross-disciplinary dependencies, and making sure the whole visual suite was optimized for production scale and the target hardware.

## Procedural fur: mesh generation

On the graphics and engine side, I built a procedural rendering pipeline in Godot to bring the character visuals to life. At its center was a custom mesh-generation tool that ingested and deserialized the original game's proprietary Unity .asset files — carrying that data across into the new engine — parsing vertex colors for fur density and UV2 four-float vectors for styling parameters. From that data it dynamically extruded and generated 31 distinct fur shell layers, preserving full skeletal blend shapes, bone weights, tangents, and UV data across every layer.

## The fur shader

To render those shells efficiently, I authored a multi-pass Godot fur shader with vertex wind displacement, fresnel rim lighting, fine-grained strand width and scale controls, and full PBR lighting integration. The real challenge was balance — holding high-fidelity visual depth against the GPU fill-rate limits of the target hardware.
